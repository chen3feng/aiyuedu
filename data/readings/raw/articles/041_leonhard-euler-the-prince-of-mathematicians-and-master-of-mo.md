Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) was a Swiss mathematician, physicist, astronomer, geometer, and engineer. Widely regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians in human history, his extraordinary productivity transformed nearly every branch of pure and applied mathematics.

Despite suffering blindess in one eye in his late twenties and total blindness later in life, Euler produced over

800 books and papers

, laying the foundation for mathematical analysis, graph theory, mechanics, and number theory.

4. Graph Theory and the Seven Bridges of Königsberg

In 1736, Euler solved the famous

Seven Bridges of Königsberg

problem, giving birth to

Graph Theory

and

Topology

.

The Puzzle:

Was it possible to walk through the city of Königsberg crossing each of its seven bridges across the Pregel River exactly once?

Euler’s Abstraction:

Euler realized that the geographical landmasses could be represented as

nodes (vertices)

and the bridges as

edges

.

The Theorem:

He proved that an Eulerian path exists if and only if a graph has either zero or exactly two vertices with an

odd degree

(odd number of connecting edges). Since all four landmasses in Königsberg had an odd number of bridges, such a walk was mathematically impossible.

6. Resilience Through Blindness and Legacy

In 1738, Euler lost sight in his right eye due to severe fever and eye strain. By 1766, a cataract in his left eye left him almost completely blind.

Remarkably, Euler's mental calculation abilities and eidetic memory allowed him to continue publishing at an unprecedented rate, dictating complex equations to his assistants. He published over half of his lifetime output during his period of total blindness.

Mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace famously summarized Euler’s monumental influence on modern science:

"Read Euler, read Euler, he is the master of us all."
