# The Turbulent French Revolution: From Enlightenment to the Republic

> 法国大革命的曲折史诗：从启蒙曙光到共和确立

The

French Revolution

is one of the most complex and dramatic transformations in human history. Far from a smooth march toward liberty, it was a century-long roller coaster of radical ideals, violence, imperial conquests, and monarchical restorations.

In the 18th century, the

Enlightenment

laid the intellectual foundation for change. Thinkers like Voltaire, Rousseau, and Montesquieu challenged divine right, advocating for liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty. Facing financial bankruptcy, King Louis XVI summoned the

Estates-General

in 1789—a assembly representing the clergy (First Estate), nobility (Second Estate), and commoners (Third Estate). When the Third Estate was outvoted, they broke away to form the National Assembly, sparking the outbreak of the Revolution with the storming of the Bastille on July 14, 1789.

The revolutionary process quickly descended into radicalism. Threatening foreign invasions and domestic counter-revolutions gave rise to

extremism

. Led by Robespierre and the Jacobins, the "Reign of Terror" executed tens of thousands by guillotine, including the King and Queen. This period proved the extreme dangers of fanaticism, showing how noble ideals of liberty can easily turn into brutal tyranny when unchecked by law and human rights.

Out of the political chaos emerged

Napoleon Bonaparte

. Seizing power in 1799, he crowned himself Emperor, codified civil law with the

Napoleonic Code

, and conquered much of continental Europe before his eventual defeat at Waterloo in 1815. What followed was a turbulent cycle: the

Bourbon Restoration

brought back absolute monarchs, leading to the 1848 revolution and the rise of

Napoleon III

(Second Empire). In 1871, following France's defeat in the Franco-Prussian War, the working-class uprising known as the

Paris Commune

briefly seized the capital before being brutally suppressed. Finally, out of these ashes, the Third Republic stably emerged.

France's repeated street protests and regime changes earned it the affectionate modern nickname "the capital of revolution" (革命老区). The legacy of this winding path is profound: it permanently destroyed feudalism in Western Europe, spread modern

republicanism

, and deeply influenced international relations—including shaping American politics as early political parties clashed over supporting French radicalism versus British stability.
