# The Panthéon: Resting Place of Giants and Dumas's Controversy

> 法国先贤祠：思想巨擘的归宿与大仲马的入祠争议

Located on the Left Bank of Paris in the historic Latin Quarter, the

Panthéon

is a striking monument dedicated to honoring the greatest figures in French history. Its grand facade bears the famous inscription:

"Aux grands hommes, la patrie reconnaissante"

—"To great men, the grateful homeland."

The building was originally commissioned by King Louis XV around 1758 as a magnificent church dedicated to Saint Genevieve, the patron saint of Paris. However, by the time construction was completed during the outbreak of the French Revolution in 1791, the Revolutionary government decided to transform the sacred church into a secular

mausoleum

to bury distinguished citizens who shaped the nation. Over the following century, as France shifted between republics and empires, the building toggled between a church and a secular memorial, before permanently remaining a national memorial hall during the Third Republic.

Entering the crypt of the Panthéon is like stepping into a living textbook of Western civilization. It houses the remains of iconic philosophers like

Voltaire

and

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

, scientists like

Marie Curie

(the first woman interred on her own merits) and Louis Pasteur, political heroes like

Victor Schoelcher

(who abolished slavery in France) and Jean Moulin (a key WWII Resistance leader), and literary titans like

Victor Hugo

and

Émile Zola

.

In 2002, the Panthéon welcomed one of France's most beloved writers:

Alexandre Dumas

(author of

The Count of Monte Cristo

and

The Three Musketeers

), marking the 200th anniversary of his birth. President Jacques Chirac ordered his remains moved from his hometown cemetery to the national monument.

However, this decision sparked considerable public

controversy

. Critics argued that Dumas was primarily a writer of popular entertainment rather than a serious political or moral thinker. More importantly, historians highlighted the underlying racial bias Dumas faced during his lifetime as a man of mixed race (his grandmother was an enslaved Afro-Caribbean woman). Opponents of the move questioned whether France was using him to parade racial inclusivity without confronting historic racism, while supporters celebrated his entry as a overdue victory against the prejudice that had demeaned his literary genius throughout the 19th century. Today, Dumas rests alongside his close friend and rival, Victor Hugo, bringing full circle the story of French literature.
