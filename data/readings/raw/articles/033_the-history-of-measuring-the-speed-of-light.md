# The History of Measuring the Speed of Light

From Infinite Speed Assumptions to the Universal Metrological Standard

For centuries, the speed of light was believed to be instantaneous. The journey from treating light as an infinite force to accurately measuring its finite speed—and eventually using it to define fundamental units of measurement—is one of the most remarkable chapters in physics and metrology.

1. Early Theories and Galileo’s Failed Lantern Experiment

In ancient and medieval times, philosophers such as Aristotle argued that light was not a moving object, but rather a presence that filled space instantaneously.

In the early 17th century,

Galileo Galilei

made one of the first recorded scientific attempts to measure light's speed:

The Experiment:

Galileo and an assistant stood on two hilltops nearly a mile apart at night, each holding a covered lantern. Galileo uncovered his lantern; as soon as his assistant saw the light, he uncovered his own. Galileo attempted to measure the time delay.

The Failure:

Human reaction time (around 200–300 milliseconds) completely dwarfed the fraction of a microsecond light actually took to travel between the hills. Galileo concluded that if light's speed was not instantaneous, it was extraordinarily fast beyond human perception.

2. Cosmic Scale Breakthroughs: Astronomical Observations

Because terrestrial distances were too short for 17th-century equipment, the first successful measurements came from observing astronomical distances.

Ole Rømer and Jupiter's Moons (1676)

Danish astronomer

Ole Rømer

noticed an anomaly while tracking the eclipses of Io, Jupiter's innermost moon.

The Observation:

When Earth was moving away from Jupiter, the time between Io's eclipses grew longer. When Earth approached Jupiter, the intervals shortened.

The Realization:

Rømer deduced that this variation occurred because light took extra time to cross the changing distance across Earth's orbit.

Using Rømer's timing data, Christiaan Huygens subsequently calculated light's speed to be approximately

220

,

000

km/s

—significantly under the true value due to inaccuracies in planetary distances at the time, but proving irrefutably that light travels at a finite speed.

James Bradley and Stellar Aberration (1728)

English astronomer

James Bradley

discovered

stellar aberration

—the apparent slight shift in the positions of stars caused by the combination of Earth's orbital velocity and the speed of light (analogous to how rain appears to fall at an angle when running). Bradley calculated the speed of light to be approximately

301

,

000

km/s

, remarkably close to the true value.

3. Terrestrial Breakthroughs: Fizeau's Toothed Wheel and Foucault's Mirror

By the mid-19th century, laboratory instruments became precise enough to measure light over earthbound distances without relying on stars.

Hippolyte Fizeau and the Toothed Wheel (1849)

French physicist

Hippolyte Fizeau

built the first terrestrial apparatus:

Light was focused through a rotating toothed wheel, traveled

8.6

km

to a stationary mirror, and reflected back toward the wheel.

At slow rotational speeds, the returning beam passed through the gaps between the teeth.

As the wheel spun faster, a returning light pulse would hit an adjacent tooth instead of a gap, blocking the view. Knowing the distance, number of teeth, and rotational speed, Fizeau calculated

c

≈

313

,

300

km/s

.

Léon Foucault and the Rotating Mirror (1862)

Léon Foucault

improved upon Fizeau's design by replacing the toothed wheel with a rotating mirror:

While the light pulse traveled to a distant fixed mirror and back, the rotating mirror moved by a fraction of a degree, deflecting the returning beam at a measurable angle.

Foucault refined the value to

298

,

000

km/s

and demonstrated that light travels slower in water than in air, providing strong experimental support for the wave theory of light.

Albert A. Michelson's Precision Optics

American physicist

Albert A. Michelson

dedicated decades to perfecting rotating-mirror and interferometry techniques. Using long, evacuated tubes and precise optical setups (most notably between Mount Wilson and Mount San Antonio in California), Michelson achieved a measurement of

299

,

796

±

4

km/s

in 1926, setting the benchmark for optical methods.

Radio Wave and Laser Interferometry (Mid-20th Century)

With the advent of radar, microwaves, and lasers, scientists stopped using direct distance-over-time measurements. Instead, they measured a wave's frequency (

f

) and wavelength (

λ

) independently, calculating speed via the wave relation:

c

=

λ

⋅

f

By using stabilized lasers and atomic clocks in the 1970s, the speed of light was measured with an uncertainty of less than 1 meter per second.

5. Modern Metrology: Defining the Meter via

c

By 1975, the uncertainty in measuring the speed of light was no longer limited by time-measurement technology, but by the physical inaccuracy of the international prototype meter bar itself.

In

1983

, the 17th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) fundamentally flipped the relationship between light and distance:

They permanently fixed the speed of light in a vacuum to an

exact constant

:

c

=

299

,

792

,

458

m/s

They redefined the

meter

based on this fixed constant and the SI second:

"The meter is the length of the path traveled by light in vacuum during a time interval of

1/299

,

792

,

458

of a second."

Because the speed of light is a fundamental constant of physics (

c

), setting its exact numerical value eliminated future errors in distance standards, anchoring spatial measurement to atomic time.
