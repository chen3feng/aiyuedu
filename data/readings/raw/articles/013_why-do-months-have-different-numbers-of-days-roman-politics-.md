# Why Do Months Have Different Numbers of Days? Roman Politics and Superstitions

> 为什么每个月的天数不一样？古罗马的政治与迷信

<!-- 来源：Gemini 对话（share.gemini.google/6bKWnzs2Ol8h） · 原文 2697 字符 · 汉字占比 8% -->

Have you ever wondered why some months have 30 days, others have 31, and February has only 28 (or 29 in a leap year)? The strange lengths of our months are not based on science or the moon; instead, they are the result of ancient Roman history, politics, and ancient superstitions!

In the earliest Roman calendar, the year had only 10 months and lasted 304 days, leaving winter as an uncounted gap. Around 700 BCE, a king named

Numa Pompilius

added

January

and

February

to cover the winter and make a 12-month year of 355 days. At that time, Romans had a strange superstition: they believed that even numbers brought bad luck! Because of this, Numa tried to make all months have

odd numbers

of days (either 29 or 31).

However, math got in the way. To make the total days add up to 355, one month had to have an even number. They chose

February

, the final month of the year dedicated to purification (

februa

), giving it 28 days. People thought bad luck wouldn't matter much in a short, purifying month!

Centuries later,

Julius Caesar

introduced a new calendar (the

Julian calendar

) to match the sun's 365-day cycle. He added days to make most months 30 or 31 days long. Later, his successor

Augustus

named August after himself. Legend says Augustus did not want his month to be shorter than July (named after Julius Caesar), so he stole a day from February and added it to August, locking February into its short 28-day shape forever!
