# Yu Qian: The Patriot Who Saved the Ming Dynasty

<!-- 来源：Gemini 对话（share.gemini.google/6bKWnzs2Ol8h） · 原文 8803 字符 · 汉字占比 1% -->

Yu Qian (1398–1457) was a prominent statesman, military strategist, and national hero of the Ming Dynasty. Celebrated alongside Yue Fei and Zhang Cangshui as one of the

"Three Heroes of West Lake,"

Yu Qian single-handedly stabilized a crumbling empire during the Tumu Crisis, orchestrating the successful Defense of Beijing and preserving the continuity of Chinese civilization.

1. Early Life and the Metaphor of "Lime"

Born in Qiantang (modern Hangzhou), Yu Qian passed the imperial civil service examination (

Jinshi

) in 1421. Throughout his administrative career across Shanxi and Henan, he was widely revered for his incorruptibility and devotion to public welfare.

When visiting the capital, officials customarily presented lavish bribes to powerful court eunuchs. Yu Qian consistently arrived empty-handed, declaring that he brought only the "fresh breeze in his sleeves"—giving rise to the Chinese idiom

两袖清风

(

Liǎng Xiù Qīng Fēng

— "having clean hands").

As a youth, he authored his famous poem

Praise to Lime

(

Shí Huī Yín

), which foretold his tragic and noble destiny:

Thousands of hammer blows and chisel strikes hew it from the deep mountains;

It views burning in the fierce fire as nothing out of the ordinary.

Unfearing of being smashed to powder and bones broken to dust,

It seeks only to leave its purity behind in the human realm.

2. The Tumu Crisis: An Empire on the Brink

In

August 1449

, the Ming Dynasty faced an existential disaster known as the

Tumu Crisis

(土木堡之变).

Disastrous Campaign:

Instigated by the corrupt eunuch Wang Zhen, Emperor Zhengtong personally led an ill-prepared military expedition against Esen Taishi, leader of the Oirat Mongols.

The Annihilation:

The Ming army was ambushed and annihilated at Tumu Fortress. The Emperor was captured alive, and the elite guard of the imperial military perished overnight.

The Factional Rift:

Panic seized the capital. Some senior ministers proposed abandoning Beijing and moving the seat of government south to Nanjing—mirroring the tragic retreat of the Song Dynasty centuries earlier.

3. The Defense of Beijing: "He Who Advocates Retreat Shall Be Executed"

Rising to the position of Minister of War, Yu Qian forcefully rejected the retreat proposals in court, declaring:

"Those who advocate retreating to the South should be executed!"

Constitutional Maneuver:

To negate Esen Taishi's political leverage in using the captive emperor as a bargaining chip, Yu Qian persuaded the imperial court to elevate the Emperor's brother, Zhu Qiyu (Emperor Jingdi), to the throne.

Desperate Mobilization:

Yu Qian requisitioned grain supplies from granaries across northern China, drafted local militias, and reorganized defense lines around Beijing's nine city gates.

No-Retreat Orders:

In October 1449, as Oirat forces reached the outskirts of Beijing, Yu Qian led troops outside Desheng Gate and ordered the gates locked behind them. He issued a strict martial code:

"If troops retreat without their commander's order, the rear line shall execute the front line."

After days of intense hand-to-hand combat and artillery bombardment, the Oirat forces were repulsed, saving Beijing and preventing the collapse of the Ming Dynasty.

4. The Coup and Tragic Martyrdom

Having failed to extort the Ming court, Esen Taishi released the former Emperor Zhengtong in 1450. The retired emperor was placed under house arrest for seven years by his reigning brother.

The Restoration Coup (1457):

Opportunistic court officials executed a night coup (

Duo Men Zhi Bian

), reinstating Emperor Zhengtong to the throne.

Fabricated Treason:

Political rivals, including Xu Youzhen and Shi Heng, falsely accused Yu Qian of treason. When the Emperor hesitated due to Yu Qian's historic merit, Xu Youzhen argued:

"If Yu Qian is not executed, your restoration lacks legal justification."

Execution:

On February 16, 1457, Yu Qian was executed outside Chongwen Gate. When investigators raided his home, they found no riches or hidden treasures—only books and imperial garments bestowed by the previous emperor.

Emperor Xianzong officially exonerated Yu Qian years later, granting him the posthumous title

Duansu

(端肃 — "Upright and Solemn"). Today, his tomb stands near Hangzhou's West Lake, revered as a monument to selflessness and moral fortitude.
