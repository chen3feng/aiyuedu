# Measuring Cosmic Distances: Unlocking the Secrets of Universe Scales

> 测量天体距离：揭开宇宙尺度的秘密

<!-- 来源：Gemini 对话（share.gemini.google/6bKWnzs2Ol8h） · 原文 2698 字符 · 汉字占比 7% -->

Measuring distances in space is one of the greatest challenges in astronomy because the universe is unimaginably huge. To describe these vast gaps, astronomers use special units instead of kilometers. The

Astronomical Unit (AU)

is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, used mainly within our solar system. For longer distances, we use the

light-year (ly)

—the distance light travels in one Earth year—and the

parsec (pc)

, which equals about 3.26 light-years.

For nearby stars, astronomers use

trigonometric parallax

. As Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift slightly against background stars. By measuring this tiny angle, scientists can calculate its exact distance using geometry.

When objects are too far away for parallax, astronomers rely on "standard candles"—objects with a known intrinsic brightness.

Cepheid variables

are a classic example: these pulsating giant stars change in brightness at a rate directly related to their true luminosity. By comparing how bright they look to how bright they actually are, scientists can find their distance.

For extreme cosmic distances across the universe, astronomers look to

supernovae

, specifically Type Ia supernovae. These powerful stellar explosions have a consistent peak brightness, acting like giant cosmic lightbulbs shining across galaxies. By stepping up from parallax to Cepheids and supernovae, astronomers have built a "cosmic distance ladder" that maps the entire universe!
